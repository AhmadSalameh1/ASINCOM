\title{Towards Trustworthy Disruption Response: Predict, Decide, Explain and Bound on an Evidence-Based
Supply-Chain Twin}

% TODO(authors): confirm the author list, order and e-mail addresses.
\author[First]{Ahmad Salameh}
\author[First]{Sara Himmiche}

\address[First]{SYMME, Universit\'e Savoie Mont Blanc, Annecy, France}% TODO(e-mail): add (e-mail: ...) as in the IFAC sample

\begin{abstract}
AI layers on supply-chain digital twins are usually trained and assessed on one calibrated simulator. We ask
what such layers can be trusted to do when every part of the twin, and every disruption injected into it, is
backed by data, and when the decisions that shaped the data change. From the SAP records of three runs of an
ERPsim game, played on the same company by the same student group with different decisions, we derive a day-level
twin, validate it on the two later runs, and generate it also as a UPPAAL model. Disruptions are sampled within
open-data and in-game evidence, and responses use only the levers the players had. Four layers predict the impact
of a notified disruption with conformal bounds, choose a response under a service limit, distil the choice into a
depth-3 tree, and bound the tree's service and harm rates with exact binomial confidence bounds. On an independent
sample of the build run, the tree raises the share of disruptions kept within one day of lost demand from 77.6 to
80.0\,\% (paired difference +2.5 points, 95\,\% CI 1.8--3.1) and does harm in 1.4\,\% ($\le$2.0\,\%). On the later
runs, coverage and decision value are lost unless the layers are recalibrated or adapted on the target twin, and
explanation stability does not reveal the loss.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keyword}
Digital twins; supply chain resilience; disruption management; conformal prediction; explainable AI; statistical
model checking; ERP data.
\end{keyword}

\section{Introduction}

Disruptions of supply, production and demand propagate through stock and capacity into lost sales
\citep{dolgui2018ripple}, and decision support for them is increasingly built as AI layers on digital twins
\citep{ivanov2021digitaltwin,ivanov2023idt,baryannis2019scrm}. Trust depends on two things rarely examined together: the twin,
which is often calibrated to whatever data exists and validated, if at all, on the data used to build it
\citep{sargent2013vv}; and the AI layer, whose predictions, decisions and explanations should come with statements
of how often they hold, and for which population.

This paper builds both from the same evidence. The twin is derived from the SAP tables of an ERPsim
manufacturing game \citep{leger2006erpsim} released as open research data \citep{tritscher2022openerp}. Three runs
of the game on the same company, played by the same group with different decisions, give a controlled test of
transfer: everything is built on the first run and checked on the other two. On this twin we stack four layers
--- predict, decide, explain, bound --- and report where they hold and where they do not.

The contributions are:
(i) an \emph{evidence-first} twin, derived from the recorded data, checked on two runs it was not built on with
metrics fixed beforehand, and also generated as a UPPAAL model;
(ii) disruptions sampled \emph{within evidence bounds} and responses restricted to the players' levers;
(iii) a predict--decide--explain--bound pipeline whose statistical guarantee is on the explanation itself; and
(iv) a measured account of transfer across decision policies, including what restores it.

\section{Related work}

Supply-chain twins for resilience range from simulation models of disruption propagation to recovery planning
by optimisation \citep{ivanov2021digitaltwin,hosseini2019resilience}; ripple-effect and viability studies stress
propagation and co-occurrence \citep{dolgui2018ripple,ivanov2023idt}, and time-to-recover and time-to-survive
models target the weak links \citep{simchilevi2015risk}. AI is used for risk prediction and response
\citep{baryannis2019scrm}. Conformal
prediction gives distribution-free coverage under exchangeability \citep{vovk2005alrw,romano2019cqr,
angelopoulos2023gentle}; under shift it has been extended by importance weighting
\citep{tibshirani2019covariateshift}, online adaptation \citep{gibbs2021aci} and weighted non-exchangeable
schemes \citep{barber2023beyond}, and to decisions by risk control and conformal decision theory
\citep{angelopoulos2024crc,lekeufack2024cdt}. Black boxes can be distilled into trees
\citep{bastani2017extraction}, which are preferable in high-stakes settings \citep{rudin2019stop} and established
as controller representations \citep{ashok2020dtcontrol}. Statistical model checking (SMC) estimates the
probability of temporal properties with confidence bounds \citep{legay2010smc}; UPPAAL SMC and Stratego support
stochastic models and strategy synthesis \citep{david2015uppaalsmc,david2015stratego}, and SMC has evaluated the
robustness of production schedules \citep{himmiche2018smc}. We combine them on a twin whose evidence and validation are part of the contribution.

\section{An evidence-first twin}

\subsection{Data and notation}
ERPsim runs a simulated company in SAP; a game \emph{round} spans 20 simulated days and a run has 12 rounds. The
released data \citep{tritscher2022openerp} contain several runs; we use the three runs of one group of five
students on the same company: Y1 (dataset \emph{normal~2}, build run), Y2 and Y3 (\emph{fraud~2},
\emph{fraud~3}), in which one participant also acted as a fraudster. The company sells one detailed product (F12)
to about 70 customers through three distribution centres (DCs: North, South, West), makes it from five of eight
components on a line of \num{24000} units per day shared with two to three other products, and buys components
with lead times of mostly one to six days. \emph{Days of demand} divide a quantity by the mean daily F12 demand of
the 20 days before the notice (about \num{7700} units in Y1); the \emph{cover} of a stock is its days of demand.
\emph{Steady months} are the months after the start-up phase; a \emph{notice} tells the planner the type and
severity of a disruption on its first day. Prices come from purchase-order (EKPO) and sales-order (VBAP) lines.

\subsection{Evidence ledger, structure and policy}
Every element is derived by scripts from the SAP tables and recorded with its source, method and value per run.
Seventeen structural claims are tested in all runs, e.g.\ unmet demand is lost, plant-to-DC transfers are
instantaneous, all products share one line, and an order starts only when the components for its whole batch are
in stock (78 of 78 starts). The players' only planning decision is the forecast; SAP MRP turns it into production
and purchase orders, and an MRP replica reproduces 20 of 21 production decisions and 29 of 37 purchasing runs.
The F12 margin is 1.81 to 2.37\,\euro{} per unit.

\subsection{Twin and validation}
The twin advances in game days: supplier receipts with sampled lead times, production on the shared line (one
line-day is \num{24000} units; a product switch costs 0.6 line-day of capacity, deducted in units and carried
over to the next day if needed), transfers, sales clipped to DC stock, and decisions. For validation the players' recorded decisions are
replayed, so that the test concerns the physics. A protocol fixed on Y1 before any validation run, and documented
in the repository, defines eight metrics. The primary one, A1, is the maximum over days of the absolute
difference between the twin's and the recorded cumulative quantity (sales, production, transfers), divided by
the recorded yearly total; it passes at 10\,\%. A run is \emph{validated} if yearly totals and A1-sales pass and
at most two of the six secondary metrics fail. Three revisions are disclosed: the first replaced band metrics
with A1 before any validation run; the second and third were made after validation results had been seen and are
therefore post hoc. The third replays a transfer that cannot ship for lack of plant stock as soon as stock allows
(the players' records show an empty plant at every year end, whereas plain replay stranded 30--72\,k units there).
With it, A1 is 0.5, 1.0 and 1.0\,\% for sales and 2.2, 3.6 and 2.1\,\% for transfers. Y1 fails one secondary metric
(one component's stock-out share, 5.8 vs 5\,\%), Y2 two (DC stock-out months and the propagation of a recorded
production pause) and Y3 four, so Y3 is not validated. The DC differences of Y2 and Y3 are a constant offset of about two days
of demand per DC, from first-month losses that the censored demand record cannot reproduce. In Y3, fraud orders also removed \num{67431} units that the demand replay, which
excludes fraud documents, does not remove (\num{2731} in Y2). Y3 is therefore used only as a shift case with an
invalid twin.

\subsection{The same twin as a UPPAAL model}
A generator writes the twin as one day-tick timed automaton whose step function executes the same day on
doubles. UPPAAL's dense time is not used: its role is an independent implementation from the same inputs and
access to SMC and Stratego. A line-by-line Python transcription of the generated functions, reading the
constants from the generated file, reproduces the twin day by day in 591 of 591 deterministic configurations
(lead times at their medians; all disruption types, all actions and the tree of Section~\ref{sec:explain};
maximum difference $10^{-10}$ units). This checks the generator, not UPPAAL itself: running the files in
\texttt{verifyta} is pending.

\section{Disruptions within evidence, and the levers}

A disruption is admitted if it acts through a mechanism of the twin and its severity comes from evidence
(Table~\ref{tab:disruptions}); external evidence is transferred only as dimensionless ratios. E1 assumes that lateness scales with lead time across
sectors. E3 is capped at the shortest of the three recorded production
pauses (25, 52 and 60 days), so that every sampled stoppage is shorter than one the game produced. E5 delays a
step that the game makes instantaneous; it is an extension beyond the validated physics, kept because its effect
is small. A type is drawn uniformly, then a severity uniformly within its range (E1 by inverse sampling of the
empirical ratio quantiles up to the 95th percentile), and the notice day uniformly over the steady months. Disruptions are single and
do not co-occur.

order's sampled lead time).}

\toprule
Disruption & Mechanism (20-day window from the notice) & Severity range (evidence)\\
\midrule

Under the players' own decisions the same 19\,\% surge costs Y1 a median of \num{28296} extra lost units, Y2
\num{5915} and Y3 none, and supplier and quality disruptions matter only at unlucky timings: resilience is a
property of the policy state, which motivates layers that observe it.

Responses may use only the players' levers: purchase orders (a component buffer of 5 or 10 days of recent
consumption), production orders (an extra F12 batch of one line-day, or F12 priority on the line by holding other
products' orders while F12 waits) and transfers (rebalancing DCs by cover). The eight actions are no response,
buffer 5, buffer 10, batch, rebalance, priority, batch + priority, and buffer 10 + batch + priority. An extra batch
ordered during a stoppage waits in the queue and runs first when the line restarts. The cost of an action is the
extra inventory capital it ties up, at material cost; no holding-cost rate is assumed. Because the line runs at
82--93\,\% load, priority moves loss to the other products, so the objective counts the lost demand of all
products.

\section{Predict, decide, explain, bound}

\subsection{Episodes}
An episode is a run, a notice day, a seed and a disruption drawn as above. The twin runs it once without the
disruption and once under the disruption for each action, with common random numbers (CRN), so all eight
outcomes are observed and every comparison is paired. The 34 features are what a planner observes at the notice
(component cover, the production queue, plant and DC cover, recent losses, the transfer backlog, and the notice's
type and severity), recorded inside the run so that no future information leaks. We generate \num{2000}
episodes per run, 600 per run with severities beyond the evidence (STRESS), and an independent certification
sample of \num{2000} Y1 episodes from a separate random stream. The layers are built on Y1 (60\,\% training,
20\,\% calibration) and tested on its held-out 20\,\% and on Y2 and Y3. Because random splits share notice days,
we repeated the Y1 analysis with splits grouped by notice day.

\subsection{L1: predict}
The target is the \emph{extra} F12 loss caused by the disruption under the players' plan, over the 60 days after
the notice, in days of demand. A gradient-boosted \citep{ke2017lightgbm} quantile model $\hat q(x)$ at level
$1-\alpha=0.9$ is corrected by split conformal prediction with the $n=400$ calibration scores
$s_i = y_i - \hat q(x_i)$:
\begin{equation}
U(x) = \hat q(x) + s_{(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil)}, \qquad \Pr\{Y \le U(X)\} \ge 1-\alpha
\end{equation}
under exchangeability \citep{romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2023gentle}.

\subsection{L2: decide}
For each action $a$, a separate quantile model with its own conformal correction gives $U_a(x)$, a bound on the
\emph{total} 60-day loss of all products (the service quantity), and a point model gives $\hat C_a(x)$, the added
inventory capital. With a service limit $L^*$ of one day of demand over 60 days,
\begin{equation}
a^*(x)=\begin{cases}
\text{no response} & \text{if } U_{\text{none}}(x)\le L^*,\\
\arg\min_{a:\,U_a(x)\le L^*} \hat C_a(x) & \text{if such } a \text{ exists},\\
\arg\min_a U_a(x) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The coverage of Eq.~(1) holds for each fixed action but not for the action selected after seeing all eight
bounds, so L2 is a heuristic; only L4 carries a guarantee. Baselines: the players' plan, each fixed action, a
lookup rule (per disruption type, the action with the lowest mean training loss), L2 with point predictions, a
twin rollout that simulates each action from the observed state four times, with fresh lead times and the demand
of the 20 days before the notice, and takes the lowest mean loss, and an oracle that knows the outcomes and takes the cheapest action meeting $L^*$, else the
action with the least loss.

\subsection{L3: explain}\label{sec:explain}
L2's choices on the build episodes are distilled into a depth-3 tree \citep{bastani2017extraction}; stability is
measured over ten bootstrap refits of the whole pipeline, and SHAP \citep{lundberg2017shap} describes L1.

\subsection{L4: bound}
The object that receives the guarantee is the tree. On $N$ episodes drawn i.i.d.\ from the episode distribution
above and never used to build anything, the number $k$ that meet the service limit is binomial; the two-sided
95\,\% Clopper--Pearson interval \citep{clopper1934} gives a lower bound on the service probability that holds
with 97.5\,\% confidence. A harm bound is computed in the same way for the share of episodes in which the tree
loses more than 0.05 days of demand above the players' plan; service and harm hold jointly at 95\,\%. These are
statistical guarantees for the twin under the sampled disruption distribution, not for real disruptions, other
severity mixes or the twin's own error. $L^*$, the tree depth, the 0.9 level and the harm threshold were fixed on
Y1 before the certification sample was generated. The generated UPPAAL model embeds the tree and samples the
notice day and disruption from the same distribution; the property is
$\Pr[\le T](\Diamond\, \mathit{done} \wedge \mathit{ok})$, where $T$ is the notice day plus 60 days, $\mathit{done}$
marks the end of the 60-day window and $\mathit{ok}$ that the loss of all products in it is at most $L^*$.

\section{Results}

\textbf{L1.} On the Y1 held-out episodes the predictor's mean absolute error is 0.22 days of demand, against
1.20 for the per-type mean and 0.94 for a model that sees only the notice; with splits grouped by notice day it is
0.38 (notice-only 0.90, per-type mean 1.18), so part of the random-split advantage came from shared notice days, but the plant
state still matters. In Y2 the advantage disappears (0.78 vs 0.77 for the notice-only model); in Y3 it shrinks
(0.51 vs 0.71). Coverage of the 90\,\% bound is 88.0\,\% on the 400 held-out episodes (standard error about 1.5
points) and 89.6\,\% on the certification sample, but falls to 84.9 and 79.0\,\% in Y2 and Y3, with the worst
disruption type at 65.8 and 59.8\,\%, and to 61.2\,\% beyond the evidence. Importance-weighted conformal prediction needs overlap,
which is absent: its bound is infinite in $\ge$99.9\,\% of the shifted episodes. Recalibrating the margin on 100 target outcomes, repeated over 200 resamples and evaluated on the
remaining episodes, gives a mean coverage of 90.4\,\% (SD 2.7, 5--95\,\% range 85.9--94.8) in Y2 and 89.8\,\% (SD
3.1) in Y3, with mean bounds of 1.04 and 0.95 days against 1.02 and 0.93 for the build-run bound.

\textbf{L2.} On the Y1 held-out episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:l2}, left) the players lose 2.26 days of demand and exceed
the limit in 24.8\,\% of episodes; L2 loses 1.99 days (paired difference $-0.28$, 95\,\% CI $-0.38$ to $-0.18$),
exceeds the limit in 19.8\,\%, acts in 34\,\% of episodes and adds 25\,k\euro{} of inventory. The oracle loses
1.91 days and exceeds the limit in 19.2\,\%: even the best action in hindsight meets the limit in only 80.8\,\% of
these episodes, because no lever repairs a long line stoppage. The lookup rule spends 124\,k\euro{} and loses
more (2.11 days). Point predictions give the same loss (1.97 days) at half the spend: on loss, the conformal
margin adds caution rather than value. On \num{1000} certification episodes the twin rollout loses 1.78 days against 1.53 for L2 and meets the limit
less often than the players (75.6 vs 77.9\,\%); given the recorded future demand instead, it matches the oracle
(83.1\,\%): its value lies in knowing demand. With grouped splits L2 still beats the players on held-out days
(1.65 vs 1.98 days). Valued at the F12 margin, the avoided loss is worth about 3.8\,k\euro{} per disruption. In
68\,\% of the certification episodes the first branch of Eq.~(2) applies and in 25\,\% the third; there the chosen
action's bound covers its outcome in only 80\,\% of cases, against 88\,\% overall.

On the same \num{1600} Y2 episodes, L2 built on Y1 is slightly worse than the players (1.44 vs 1.40 days; +0.04,
CI +0.00 to +0.07): in Y2 component buffers protect the other products, which never happened in Y1. Adapting L2
with 400 Y2 episodes (300 training, 100 calibration, disjoint from the test episodes) gives 1.19 days (paired
$-0.21$, CI $-0.27$ to $-0.15$) at 60\,k\euro{}; the best fixed action loses less (1.02) but ties up
147\,k\euro{}. In Y3 there is little to gain: the players lose 0.66 days, and neither L2 nor the tree improves on that.

\textbf{L3.} The tree (Fig.~\ref{fig:tree}) has eight leaves and six features. It first asks whether the line
has stopped; if so, it orders an extra batch or a component buffer, depending on cover. Otherwise it rebalances
the DCs under a strong surge, gives F12 priority when plant stock is high, and else does nothing (75\,\% of
episodes). It agrees with L2 on 77\,\% of Y1 held-out decisions (L2 does
nothing in 66\,\%), 69\,\% in Y2 and 56\,\% in Y3. Over ten bootstrap refits the root split is stable but the lower
splits vary (median agreement 77\,\% in Y1, 54\,\% in Y3). The
SHAP ranking of L1 (stoppage length, finished-goods cover, North DC cover, surge factor first) is almost identical
in every run (Spearman 0.96--0.99) although L1's accuracy and coverage degrade: \emph{a stable explanation of a
fixed model is not evidence that the model transfers}.

\textbf{L4.} On the independent Y1 sample (Fig.~\ref{fig:cert}), the players meet the limit in 77.6\,\% of
episodes ($\ge$75.7\,\%) and the tree in 80.0\,\% ($\ge$78.2\,\%). On the same episodes the tree is better in 50
and worse in 1 (exact McNemar $p<10^{-13}$; paired difference +2.5 points, CI 1.8--3.1), and it does harm in
1.4\,\% ($\le$2.0\,\%). The black-box L2 reaches 82.7\,\%, so the explanation costs 2.6 points (CI 1.9--3.5). On
the \num{1600} Y2 test episodes, the tree built on Y1 gives no gain (78.6 vs 78.9\,\%, $p=0.18$) and does harm in
6.1\,\% ($\le$7.3\,\%); the adapted tree reaches 85.9\,\% ($\ge$84.1\,\%; +6.9 points, CI 5.5--8.3) with harm
$\le$7.0\,\%, so adaptation restores value but not a low harm rate. In Y3, whose twin is not validated, neither
tree changes service (91.3 and 91.4 vs 91.1\,\%) and both lose slightly more than the players (+0.02 days, CI
+0.00 to +0.05). Against line stoppages the certified service of every policy stays between 20 and 49\,\%: an extra batch
shortens the recovery but cannot replace lost line-days. The Python execution of the generated model gives the same service
rates within sampling error (players 78.3\,\%, tree 80.0\,\% in Y1; adapted tree 87.7\,\% in Y2, two-proportion
tests, all six $p \ge 0.11$), which supports the implementation but is not independent evidence of validity.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What can be trusted.} Within its population, the readable policy improves service with a bounded low
harm rate, using the players' own levers. When the decisions behind the twin change, the conformal coverage, the decision value and
the harm rate all degrade. A bound on the build run cannot detect this; bounds on the target twin did, and
explanation stability did not. Two repairs work: recalibrating the conformal margin on about 100 target outcomes,
and adapting the decision layer with a few hundred episodes of the target twin. Both need a validated twin of the
current policy, so the practical rule is to recalibrate or adapt when the policy state shifts, for instance when
the coverage of L1 on newly observed outcomes drifts, as in adaptive conformal inference \citep{gibbs2021aci}.
Validation down to the replay rules is a precondition: on episodes from plain transfer replay, shipping the
stranded plant stock is best in every run, and coverage (89.5--94.8\,\%) and gains (43--58\,\%) seem to transfer.

\textbf{Limitations.} The data come from a business game played by one student group, with censored demand and
injected fraud; severity evidence is partly external, and disruptions are single; the guarantees hold under our
sampling distribution only; Y3 is not validated; the other products are modelled at planning level; the cost
ignores holding rates; and the UPPAAL files have not yet been executed in \texttt{verifyta}. The tree is a
learned policy, not a strategy synthesised by Stratego \citep{david2015stratego}; synthesis over the same eight
actions, exported as a tree \citep{ashok2020dtcontrol}, is the natural next baseline.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

On a twin whose every part is backed by data and checked on runs it was not built on, a readable policy with
a statistical guarantee improves service on its build run; when the decisions change it does not, and bounds
computed on the target twin show it. Recalibration and adaptation on the target twin restore coverage
and value. Trustworthy AI for disruption response therefore depends as much on the evidence behind the twin and on
where guarantees are evaluated as on the learning method.

\textbf{Data and code availability.} The SAP data are open \citep{tritscher2022openerp}. Scripts, evidence ledger, validation
reports, twin, UPPAAL models, episodes and AI layers (with settings and seeds) will be public on publication.
